<h2>Starting from an unfamiliar world</h2>
<p>Moving from a medical background to Computer Engineering was one of the hardest transitions of my life. Throughout my earlier studies, my entire academic foundation had been built around biology, chemistry, and the kind of memorization-heavy learning that medical subjects usually demand. Stepping into an engineering program meant walking into a completely different intellectual world, one built on logic, mathematics, and abstract technical thinking rather than the descriptive style I was used to. I had entered a field where I had almost no prior understanding of programming, electronics, or technical subjects, so everything felt unfamiliar from the very beginning. Even the vocabulary used in early lectures felt foreign, as if I was learning a new language before I could even begin learning the actual subject matter.</p>

<h2>Falling behind classmates</h2>
<p>Concepts that were normal and almost intuitive for other students often felt completely new to me, and that difference made the early days genuinely challenging. Classmates who had a background in computer science or had been exposed to basic programming during their intermediate studies seemed to grasp ideas far more quickly than I did. I struggled to understand even some basic topics, the kind that were treated as starting points rather than obstacles by everyone else around me. This created a steady undercurrent of frustration, and there were moments where I sincerely questioned whether I had made the wrong choice by leaving a medical-oriented path for something so unfamiliar.</p>

<h2>Adjusting to a new way of thinking</h2>
<p>In addition to the academic pressure, I also had to adjust to an entirely new environment. The teaching methods were different, the pace was faster, and the overall style of thinking required was far more technical than anything I had encountered before. Where medical subjects often rewarded careful memorization and detailed explanation, engineering subjects rewarded structured problem solving and the ability to apply abstract concepts to concrete situations. It often felt as if I had started my education all over again from zero, except this time without the comfort of familiar study habits that had worked for me in the past.</p>

<h2>The struggle with specific subjects</h2>
<p>One of the subjects that highlighted this gap most clearly in the beginning was basic programming. While many of my classmates already had some exposure to writing simple code, I was learning concepts like variables, loops, and logical conditions for the very first time, often having to read the same explanation several times before it actually made sense. Electronics and digital logic created a similar challenge, since they required a way of thinking about circuits and signals that had no real equivalent in anything I had studied before. Every new topic felt like it was building on a foundation I did not yet have, which made even small assignments feel disproportionately difficult compared to what they probably should have been.</p>

<h2>The emotional weight of falling behind</h2>
<p>This situation was not easy emotionally, because learning slowly while watching others move faster has a quiet way of chipping away at confidence. It is one thing to struggle privately with a difficult subject, but it is another thing entirely to struggle visibly in a classroom full of peers who appear to understand the material with ease. There were days when I left lectures feeling genuinely discouraged, wondering if the gap between where I was and where I needed to be was simply too wide to close. Comparing myself to classmates who had a head start became one of the more damaging habits I had to slowly unlearn during this period.</p>

<h2>Finding small wins</h2>
<p>What slowly helped was finding small, manageable wins rather than trying to close the entire gap all at once. Instead of comparing my overall understanding to classmates who were clearly ahead, I started focusing on smaller goals, understanding one concept properly before moving to the next, finishing one assignment without help before attempting a harder one, and tracking progress in a way that was specific to my own starting point rather than someone else's. These small wins did not eliminate the difficulty, but they gave me enough evidence that improvement was actually happening, even when it did not always feel that way day to day.</p>

<h2>Leaning on support</h2>
<p>Support from a few patient classmates and occasional guidance from instructors also made a meaningful difference during this stretch. Being willing to ask repeatedly for clarification, even when it felt uncomfortable, turned out to be far more useful than quietly struggling alone and hoping things would eventually click on their own. Over time, the subjects that had once felt completely foreign started to feel merely difficult, which was a noticeable improvement from where I had started.</p>

<h2>What this period taught me</h2>
<p>Despite all of this, I did not let those difficulties fully break my motivation. I made a conscious decision to keep trying, even on days when progress felt almost invisible. Although progress came slowly, this period taught me something that no single course could have taught me on its own. It taught me patience, both with the material and with myself, and it taught me adaptability in a way that pure academic success never could have. It showed me that even when we begin from a weak position relative to others around us, consistent effort and a willingness to keep showing up can gradually close gaps that initially seem impossible to close.</p>

<p>Looking back, none of this progress happened quickly, and there was no single moment where everything suddenly became easy. It was a gradual accumulation of small efforts, repeated questions, and quiet persistence that eventually added up to real, noticeable improvement. This difficult transition from a medical background into engineering was not simply a setback to survive. It became one of the clearest examples in my entire academic journey of how determination, applied steadily over time, can carry someone through a beginning that feels far harder than anyone else's.</p>
