<h2>A carefree beginning</h2>
<p>Looking back at my college days now, from where I currently stand as a Computer Engineering student, it almost feels like I am remembering a completely different version of myself. College life, for me, was simple, light, and largely free from the kind of pressure that university would later introduce. I attended classes only three days a week, which left me with an unusually generous amount of free time compared to what I would eventually face. That schedule shaped not just my routine but my entire mindset during that period. I was comfortable, relaxed, and honestly, quite content with how things were unfolding around me.</p>
<p>During those days, my biggest concern was rarely academic. Studies did not feel overwhelming, assignments were manageable, and exams came and went without leaving any lasting scars. Because of this ease, I never really stopped to think about what was coming next. I assumed, almost without questioning it, that life would simply continue in this same comfortable rhythm. There was no urgency pushing me to think seriously about admissions, career paths, or the kind of discipline that higher education would eventually demand. My attention, instead, was focused on enjoying the moment, spending time with friends, pursuing casual hobbies, and generally living without much weight on my shoulders.</p>

<h2>The comfort trap</h2>
<p>In hindsight, this relaxed approach was not necessarily a mistake, but it was certainly a blind spot. When you are inside a comfortable routine, it becomes very easy to assume that routine is permanent. I did not fully grasp how different university life would be from college life. The structure, the intensity, the expectations, all of it existed somewhere far ahead in my mind, almost theoretical, almost unreal. I had heard seniors and older cousins talk about how demanding university could be, but those stories never fully registered as something that would apply to me anytime soon. It is strange how easily we convince ourselves that challenges belong to someone else's timeline, not our own.</p>

<h2>When the shift began</h2>
<p>As my intermediate studies started approaching their final stretch, something subtle began to shift inside me. It was not a dramatic moment or a single defining event, but rather a slow, creeping realization that this comfortable phase of my life was temporary. The closer I got to finishing intermediate, the more frequently certain thoughts started entering my mind, thoughts I had previously pushed aside without much consideration. Questions about university admissions started appearing more often. Conversations at home began shifting toward higher education and what direction I should take next. Family expectations, which had always existed quietly in the background, suddenly felt more present and more real. Career responsibilities, something I had barely thought about before, started occupying space in my daily thinking.</p>
<p>This was the first time I genuinely felt a shift within myself, a kind of internal alarm going off after a long period of silence. It was not panic, but it was definitely a new kind of awareness. I began to understand, slowly but clearly, that the comfortable routine I had enjoyed for years was not something that would continue indefinitely. A different and noticeably harder phase of life was waiting just ahead of me, and unlike college, it would not be forgiving toward casual effort or last-minute preparation. University, as I was beginning to understand it, would demand discipline, sustained focus, and a level of mental maturity that my relaxed college years had never really required from me.</p>

<h2>What this realization taught me</h2>
<p>Looking back, I think this realization mattered far more than it seemed at the time. It marked a quiet but important turning point in how I viewed my own responsibilities. Until that moment, I had been thinking like someone with unlimited time and no real deadlines. Afterward, I started thinking, even if only slightly, like someone preparing to step into a more demanding environment. The carefree mindset that had defined my college years began to give way to a more serious, more forward-looking version of myself. I started paying closer attention to conversations about university life, course structures, and what kind of effort would actually be required once I got there.</p>
<p>This shift did not happen overnight, and I will be honest, I was still far from fully prepared when university actually began. But that moment of realization, somewhere near the end of my intermediate studies, planted an important seed. It made me aware that comfort is not the same as preparation, and that enjoying a relaxed routine does not automatically translate into readiness for what comes next. I had spent years assuming that things would simply work out because they always had, without ever stress-testing that assumption against something genuinely demanding.</p>

<h2>Carrying this lesson forward</h2>
<p>As I now write this as part of my ongoing portfolio journey, documenting my path through Computer Engineering at university, this memory feels like the natural starting point. It represents the calm before a very different kind of storm, one filled with assignments, quizzes, late nights, and lessons that would eventually force me to grow in ways my college years never demanded. Understanding where I started, mentally and emotionally, makes it much easier to appreciate how far I have come since then, and how much further I still have to go.</p>
