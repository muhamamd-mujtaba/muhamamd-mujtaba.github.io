The week that exposed my routine

The pressure of quizzes and assignments did not arrive suddenly in one day. It built slowly, almost quietly, until I realized that my routine was not strong enough for university life. In college I could survive by studying close to exams, but in Computer Engineering that habit started failing very quickly. Every week had something waiting: a quiz, a lab task, an assignment, a class test, or a topic that needed revision before the next lecture. I learned that university does not only test memory. It tests consistency, and consistency was exactly the area where I was weak.

The last week before exams became especially difficult. We had pending assignments along with quizzes from subjects like DLD, Database Systems, and OOP. The day often started early, and by the time I reached home I was already tired. There were moments when we hardly found proper time to eat because the mind was constantly jumping from one task to another. At that stage I understood that the workload felt heavy not only because teachers gave work, but because I had delayed preparation. The lesson was direct and uncomfortable: if I do not prepare earlier, the same work becomes double pressure later.

Database pressure and practical thinking

Database Systems was one of the courses taught by Dr. Bilal Ahmad, and that course made me understand why technical subjects need regular practice. A database topic may look simple in a lecture, but when it appears in a quiz or assignment, the student has to know how tables, relationships, keys, and queries connect logically. Dr. Bilal Ahmad did not present Database Systems as a dry subject. He connected it with real information management and explained why organized data matters in actual systems. That changed my view because I started seeing a database as more than a few tables in a diagram.

His background in AI, machine learning, and deep learning also made this connection stronger. When he talked about real-world datasets, especially medical datasets collected with high precision and accuracy, it became clear that good machine learning cannot exist without good data. A model depends on the quality, structure, and meaning of the dataset behind it. This helped me understand why Database Systems was important even when I was tired of assignments. The subject was not separate from modern AI work; it was one of the foundations behind it. That realization made the pressure feel more purposeful.

Time management as a real skill

Before this semester, I used to think time management meant making a timetable. During this phase, I learned that time management means making decisions before pressure forces them on you. If an assignment is due after three days, it should not be started on the last night. If a quiz is expected, revision should not wait until the morning. These ideas sound simple, but following them requires discipline. I failed at that many times, and each failure showed me the same lesson again. Planning is not decoration; it is survival in a demanding semester.

I also learned that not all work has equal weight. Some tasks need deep understanding, while others need careful completion. Programming practice cannot be handled like theory reading. Database queries need hands-on solving. DLD concepts need repeated revision. OOP requires understanding examples. When I treated all tasks the same way, I wasted time. Slowly, I began to separate urgent work from important work and started giving more time to the subjects where my foundation was weaker. This was not a perfect improvement, but it was a start.

How pressure changed my mindset

The pressure of that semester made me more serious about my digital portfolio as well. When Dr. Bilal Ahmad suggested that we build a GitHub portfolio and write our semester experiences, I first saw it as another assignment. Later, I understood its value. A portfolio records the process behind growth. It shows that I did not only complete quizzes and assignments, but also reflected on why they were difficult and what they taught me. Writing about academic pressure made me more aware of my own weak habits, especially late preparation and poor task planning.

When health interrupted the semester

Illness during exams is one of those experiences that a student never plans for, but when it happens, it affects everything at once. During my mid exams, I faced a serious health issue and had to miss two important papers. At that moment, the fear was not only about being sick. The bigger fear was academic. I kept thinking about marks, missed chances, delayed papers, and how quickly I might fall behind my classmates. The semester was already difficult, so this health problem felt like another weight placed on an already tired mind.

Missing an exam is different from missing a normal class. A class can be covered later through notes or help from friends, but an exam carries pressure because it directly affects grades. I remember feeling helpless because I wanted to appear in the papers, but my body was not supporting me. This created a strange conflict inside me. One side wanted to push through the weakness, while the other side knew that health had to come first. That conflict made the situation emotionally exhausting.

The pressure after missing papers

After missing those papers, I had to deal with uncertainty. I did not know exactly how the delayed exams would be managed, how much time I would get, or whether I would be able to prepare again with the same focus. The mind becomes restless in such situations. Even while resting, I was not fully relaxed because I kept thinking about the academic consequences. Student life often looks simple from the outside, but inside it there are moments where health, pressure, family expectations, and personal fear all meet at once.

This period taught me that resilience is not always dramatic. Sometimes resilience means accepting that a situation has gone out of your control and still choosing not to give up. I could not change the fact that I became ill. I could only control what I did after recovery. When my health improved, I appeared in the missed papers and tried to complete what had been delayed. The result was less important than the fact that I returned to the process. That return gave me confidence.

Learning from Dr. Bilal Ahmad's approach

In Programming Fundamentals and Database Systems, Dr. Bilal Ahmad often pushed us to think beyond surface-level answers. His teaching style was strict, but it also trained us to face difficult situations with a practical mindset. He is experienced in AI, machine learning, and deep learning model training, and one idea from that field stayed with me during this health challenge: systems improve through repeated correction. A model does not become accurate in one attempt. It goes through errors, evaluation, adjustment, and retraining.

I started seeing my own academic journey in a similar way. Missing two papers felt like an error in my semester, but it did not mean the whole journey had failed. It meant that I had to adjust, recover, and continue. This mindset helped me reduce the fear of one bad phase. It also connected with the way Dr. Bilal Ahmad talks about real-world problems and useful datasets, especially medical datasets that are collected with precision. Health itself became part of my academic lesson, because I personally experienced how human conditions can interrupt even the best plans.
